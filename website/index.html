02 Why

We have more access to the world's music than any generation before us. But hearing a culture's music isn't the same as understanding it.

I created SoundMap to change this. SoundMap turns music into a passport to the world.

Streaming puts the world's music one tap away, yet almost nothing helps students understand who made it or how traditions shaped each other. Every place you listen your way into becomes a stamp, and every stamp comes with the story of the people behind the sound.

    07 The research

    A study from Portugal convinced me this could matter far beyond music class.

    Researchers worked with 229 sixth-graders in public schools around Lisbon, all from light-skinned families. They measured the students' prejudice toward darker-skinned people in two ways: a questionnaire, and an Implicit Association Test, which detects unconscious bias people may not notice in themselves.

    Half the students then spent six months in a school music programme that mixed Cape Verdean songs with Portuguese ones. Cape Verde is an African country and former Portuguese colony, and many Cape Verdeans live in the Lisbon area.

    The results surprised me. Prejudice dropped among the students who learned the songs, on both the questionnaire and the unconscious test. Students who had their usual classes showed no change. When the researchers checked again three months later, and then two years later, the effect was still there. It was also specific: attitudes toward Cape Verdeans improved, but attitudes toward other groups didn't. Students warmed to the people whose music they had actually learned.

    08 Respect

    Respect is a design decision.

    Early on, it was easy to describe a tradition as if it belonged to the past. I learned to present every tradition as living music made by real people today, to explain it in its own terms, and to give credit to the communities behind it.

    1. 01

      Living traditions

      Every lesson is written in the present tense. Mbira is still played at ceremonies in Zimbabwe, and gamelan sets in Bali still belong to village associations.

    2. 02

      In their own terms

      Kumbengo and birimintingo for the kora, compás for flamenco, a raga for its hour. Students learn the words musicians use, not a translation into someone else's theory.

    3. 03

      Credit by name

      The jali of the Mande, Tia Ciata's circle in Rio, the Yolŋu makers of the yidaki. Some Yolŋu songs and designs belong to particular families and need permission, and the lesson says so.

    4. 04

      Every sound is ours

      No recordings and no photos of real people. Every sound in SoundMap, and on this page, is synthesized by our own code. That's why SoundMap never plays a groove without the story of who made it.

    09 Next

    Version 2.0

    Version 2.0 would move SoundMap from learning about other cultures toward connecting with them.

    • Classroom-to-classroom listening exchanges. Paired classes in different countries record music from their own communities and place each other's on the map.
    • Voices from the tradition. Short, permission-based introductions from musicians and community members, so cultures speak for themselves.
    • "Add your family's music." Students contribute a song from their own heritage to a moderated class map.
    • Live classroom mode. A PIN lobby for teacher-hosted rounds, plus a round where the whole class keeps one rhythm in sync.
    • More languages. Lessons for students who are still learning English, and for classrooms beyond the U.S.
    • Measuring real impact. A short before-and-after survey, because research suggests music can do this, and I'd rather test it than assume it.
